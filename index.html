Be the one a buyer can check.
And the one who answers first.

Your buyer sends the same request to three suppliers, and he starts by ruling out the ones he cannot check. He looks for a street address, a listing in the manufacturer’s own dealer locator, reviews, and someone who will actually hang the panels. Your site shows him none of them. Among the suppliers who survive that, the first real price usually wins — reply within the hour and you are nearly seven times more likely to reach the person who decides than if you wait an hour longer. (Harvard Business Review measured that across 1.25 million sales leads.) Forty-eight pages of your site are already live and your configurator already quotes, so what is missing is not design: it is proof a buyer can check, installers who take the job, a product list Google can see, and one clear promise on how fast you quote, in place of the four your site makes today.

Your site promises four different quote times

Your configurator, on the screen where a buyer hands over his contact details, promises shop drawings and a quote within 2 hours; its confirmation screen says 2 business hours. Your contact and service-area pages say one business day. Your homepage says 24 to 48 hours, in five separate places. That is four promises, up to twenty-four times apart. A buyer who sees 2 hours on one page and 48 on the next stops trusting both. Whichever number you actually keep to, the other three have to go.

What your buyer can check, and what he cannot

Your homepage claims Authorized Distributor, All Major Brands, and the manufacturer badges are there to back it. A badge on your page is you saying it. A listing in Bobrick’s dealer locator is Bobrick saying it, and that is where a careful buyer looks next. He also wants a street address, years in business, reviews, and a trade association listing. Today your site has none of them.

04

How a facilities manager finds you

A facilities manager searches by the building he runs, not by the city he already knows he is in. He types “bathroom partitions for schools” or “hospital restroom partitions”, and the page that answers his questions gets the quote request. These pages rank on their own, and they qualify the lead before it reaches you: by the time he asks for a price, he has already read what you recommend for his building and why. That is why job-type pages come before city pages in this plan.

You already have six industry pages, and Mark built them well: question-and-answer sections and a budget range per stall. In month one we check each of them against the manufacturer spec sheets and the rules that apply to that building type. From month two we bring every page up to the same standard and add the building types you have no page for, hospitals first, then churches, hotels, retail and municipal buildings. We take them in the order your buyers search for them, and we show you that order before we start.

What every industry page carries

The questions that building asks first. Vandal resistance and privacy for schools. Hygiene, ADA clearances and privacy for hospitals. Moisture and rust for gyms, pools and locker rooms. Durability and a fast install for restaurants. Code compliance and budget for municipal buildings.
Your recommendation. The material and mounting style you recommend for that building, and the reason, linked to your material pages.
A budget range per stall, and typical lead times.
Real jobs of that type, with photos, as your installers send them in.
A straight path to a price. Your configurator and your one quote-speed promise, on every industry page.
A listing request in the directories where that trade looks for suppliers, so the same buyer finds you off your site too.
